<div class="civic-tech-content">

  <p>
    As important as the tool or service you’re building is, the team dynamic matters just as much. Good organization helps keep contributors aligned, makes it easier for people to participate, and gives projects the structure they need to keep moving.
  </p>

  <p>
    This guide covers the basics of organizing your project, building your team, and making the most of Project Night.
  </p>

  <h2 id="organize-your-project">Organize Your Project</h2>

  <p>
    Organization is fundamental. Without it, good intentions and talented contributors get lost. This section includes the basics every project should have in place.
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>
      <strong>You should have a one-pager for the team</strong> that outlines your mission statement and the skills needed. While this information already exists on the website or in the project pitch, newcomers find it easier when it’s consolidated in a single place. It should also include links to your Slack, GitHub, and any software to download.
    </li>

    <li>
      You should have a <strong>sense of the roles and responsibilities</strong> that your project needs. When people know what skills are needed and what’s expected, they’re more likely to find their space and commit longer to the team. This can live on a project board in Slack, where it’s visible to everyone and should be updated as the project evolves.
    </li>

    <li>
      You should keep a <strong>running meeting notes list to stay effective</strong> and know what is going on. Even brief bullet points after each session, such as decisions made, blockers raised, and next steps assigned, keep everyone on track and prevent work from being repeated.
    </li>

    <li>
      You should have <strong>some cadence of meetings</strong>. This can just be the regular Project Nights, or it can be occasional virtual meetings. Some contributors only want to work during Project Nights, and some want to work a lot in between. As a Project Lead, you should adjust based on the desires of the team and time-sensitive milestones you have.
    </li>
  </ul>

:::note 
    CIB Mango Tree has weekly meetings, each for their product and engagement teams. The meetings are designed to help with accomplishing tasks and also serve as a good place for people to learn new tasks between project nights. However, it is not expected that you as a project need to do this. Find a rhythm that works for your team’s size and bandwidth.
:::

  <h2 id="build-your-team">Build Your Team</h2>

  <p>
    Building a healthy community means making it easy for people to show up and feel like their contribution matters.
  </p>

  <h3 id="supporting-new-contributors">Supporting new contributors</h3>

  <ul>
    <li>
      Start by lowering the barrier to entry. Have a one-pager available that explains the project’s purpose and how to get set up locally. This should be something someone can read in five minutes and walk away from knowing what the project is, why it matters, and what they can do today.
    </li>

    <li>
      Mark beginner-friendly topics as good first issue. These should be real, self-contained tasks — not busywork — that give a new contributor a genuine win and a feel for how the project operates.
    </li>

    <li>
      Make sure to have an onboarding process, whether that’s a walkthrough during Project Nights, a one-pager checklist of tasks, or a personal onboarding session for contributors who want to go deeper. A clear path in prevents the drop-off that happens when someone is interested but doesn’t know where to start.
    </li>

    <li>
      As a project lead, it is also expected that you can’t give all of your time to new contributors. If they request extra time that you can’t commit to, be gentle with them. Point them to documentation, connect them with another team member who can help, and let them know you appreciate their enthusiasm. Honesty about your capacity is better than overpromising.
    </li>

    <li>
      Respond to first pull requests and issues within 48 hours. A quick acknowledgment goes a long way toward turning a one-time visitor into a regular contributor. Even “I’ll review this by end of week” is better than silence.
    </li>
  </ul>

  <h3 id="recognizing-strengths">Recognizing strengths</h3>

  <p>
    Civic tech projects attract a wide range of people. Ask what someone <em>wants</em> to do, not just what their resume says. A policy analyst might want to try their hand at data wrangling; a developer might want to write documentation.
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>
      Create roles that match people’s goals, not just their credentials. Have them visible on a Slack board so anyone can see what’s available and self-select into work that fits them. Named roles — even informal ones like “data lead” or “community liaison” — give people something to grow into.
    </li>

    <li>
      Public recognition signals that people’s time is valued and encourages others to step up:
      <ul>
        <li>
          Have a contributor give the project pitch at Project Nights. It is a good way for them to practice their public speaking skills and succinctly deliver a quick pitch — and it visibly signals that leadership isn’t concentrated in one person.
        </li>

        <li>
          Give a thank-you in the project’s Slack channel when someone ships something, joins a call, or helps onboard a new member. Specific recognition (“thanks to Maya for cleaning up that data pipeline”) lands better than generic praise.
        </li>

        <li>
          Allow people to take “ownership” of part of the project. When someone feels responsible for a feature, a dataset, or a relationship with a partner, they show up differently. Ownership builds accountability without hierarchy.
        </li>
      </ul>
    </li>
  </ul>

  <h2 id="run-project-night">Run Project Night</h2>

  <h3 id="project-pitch">Project Pitch</h3>

  <ul>
    <li>
      This is the best time to share about your project. There is a room full of people who care about civic tech and are actively looking for something to contribute to.
    </li>

    <li>
      <strong>Please keep it to 30 seconds or a minute.</strong> The longer you take, the less time you have for the actual project work. A tight pitch also signals that you respect everyone’s time, which earns goodwill. Practice saying it out loud before the event so it feels natural.
    </li>

    <li>
      Update the master slides frequently so the information is current. Here is the link.
    </li>
  </ul>

  <h3 id="establishing-a-rhythm">Establishing a Rhythm</h3>

  <ul>
    <li>
      It is up to you how you structure your project nights. Some project leads jump right into the tasks, and when new members join after new member orientation, they move into introductions then. Others use the first few minutes for a quick standup (what got done since last time, what’s happening tonight, where help is needed).
    </li>

    <li>
      Some project nights do not do any new member orientation due to time constraints. In those cases, it is recommended to have a one-pager that new members can look at to get onboarded on their own. However, there should be some acknowledgment of new members that involves the entire team. Even a brief “welcome, glad you’re here” from the group goes a long way or a follow-up directly afterwards.
    </li>

    <li>
      Don’t dominate the conversation during project nights. It’s good to let other people lead and talk about what they are working on. Contributors feel more invested when they have space to share progress and own parts of the conversation. Your job as Project Lead is to lead, but also know when to let others have the space.
    </li>
  </ul>

  <h3 id="next-steps">Next Steps</h3>

  <p>
    New member enthusiasm wanes VERY quickly after a meeting, so it's recommended to check in with them and write a welcome message in Slack within 24 hours of the event. Reference something specific from the conversation, such as what they said they were interested in or what issue they were going to look at, so it feels personal rather than automated.
  </p>
