Civic tech projects are built by people volunteering their time, often alongside full-time jobs, school, family, and other commitments. Unlike traditional workplaces, project teams don't have formal reporting structures or guaranteed availability. People come and go, priorities change, and sometimes a project doesn't move forward as planned.

These challenges are normal in volunteer-led work. What matters is how your team responds to them. Addressing problems early, communicating openly, and being realistic about what people can contribute can help prevent small frustrations from becoming larger issues.

This guide covers some common challenges Civic Tech DC projects may encounter and ways to work through them.

## 1. When Contributors Stop Showing Up

Volunteer participation naturally fluctuates. Someone might attend three Project Nights in a row and then disappear for a month. Others may be enthusiastic about joining but never make their first contribution.

**What you can do:**

- **Check in without creating pressure.** A friendly Slack message asking how someone is doing can go a long way. Avoid making people feel guilty about stepping away.
- **Make it easy to return.** Keep project documentation and tasks up to date so contributors don't have to catch up on weeks of conversations.
- **Avoid depending on one person.** Where possible, document important decisions, share access to project resources, and have more than one person familiar with critical parts of the project.
- **Plan around actual capacity.** A team of ten Slack members doesn't necessarily mean ten active contributors. Set milestones based on who's consistently participating.

**Remember:** A volunteer stepping away isn't necessarily a reflection of the project. The goal is to create an environment where people can contribute meaningfully when they have the capacity.

## 2. When a Project Lead Steps Away

Project leads are often the people holding everything together. They coordinate meetings, maintain relationships with community partners, and help contributors understand what needs to happen next.

When a lead becomes unavailable, a project can lose momentum quickly, particularly if important information or responsibilities haven't been shared.

**What you can do:**

- **Reach out privately.** Ask whether the lead needs a break, additional support, or wants to transition out of the role.
- **Identify what needs immediate attention.** Are there upcoming commitments to a community partner? Is someone maintaining the repository? Are contributors waiting for decisions?
- **Explore shared leadership.** Another contributor may be willing to coordinate meetings or take responsibility for a specific part of the project without becoming the sole project lead.
- **Document the transition.** Make sure the next lead has access to relevant files, accounts, contacts, and outstanding tasks.
- **Know when to pause.** If no one is available to take responsibility, it may be better to temporarily pause the project rather than continue making commitments that the team cannot meet.

If your project is at risk of losing its lead, reach out to Civic Tech DC organizers early. We can help discuss possible next steps, although we cannot guarantee that another volunteer will be available to take over.

## 3. When a Community Partner Becomes Unresponsive

Community partners help ensure that projects address real needs. They provide context, connect volunteers with users, and help validate whether proposed solutions will actually work.

But partners have competing responsibilities too. Staff turnover, changing organizational priorities, and limited capacity can make it difficult to maintain consistent communication.

**What you can do:**

- **Clarify what you need.** Instead of sending a general request for feedback, ask a specific question or identify a decision that needs to be made.
- **Agree on communication expectations.** Discuss how often the team needs feedback, who the primary contact is, and what decisions require partner approval.
- **Explain the consequences of delays.** If the team cannot move forward without feedback, communicate that clearly and respectfully.
- **Avoid making assumptions.** Don't continue developing major features based on what you think the partner wants if you haven't been able to validate those decisions.
- **Consider pausing the work.** If a partner remains unavailable after repeated attempts to reconnect, discuss whether the project can reasonably continue.

Remember that community partnerships are relationships, not transactions. The goal isn't to pressure partners into responding faster, but to establish expectations that respect everyone's time.

## 4. When Contributors Disagree on Project Direction

Disagreements are inevitable when people with different backgrounds, experiences, and priorities work together. A developer might want to rebuild a tool using a different framework, while a community partner wants to focus on making the existing version easier to use.

Disagreement itself isn't necessarily a problem. It can lead to better decisions when people have space to explain their reasoning.

**What you can do:**

- **Return to the problem.** Ask who the project is intended to serve and whether the proposed direction addresses their needs.
- **Separate preferences from requirements.** A particular technology or design approach may be someone's preference rather than something the project actually needs.
- **Make decisions transparent.** Explain how a decision was reached, particularly when not everyone's preferred approach is chosen.
- **Clarify decision-making responsibilities.** Project leads should help coordinate decisions, but community partners should have a meaningful voice in choices that affect their organizations and users.
- **Know when to involve organizers.** If a disagreement becomes personal, repeatedly disrupts work, or cannot be resolved within the team, reach out to Civic Tech DC organizers.

Disagreements about technical choices or project priorities are different from harassment, discrimination, or other violations of our [Code of Conduct](https://www.civictechdc.org/code-of-conduct). Those concerns should be brought to organizers rather than handled solely as ordinary project disagreements.

## 5. When Your Project Keeps Growing in Scope

It's easy for a civic tech project to expand beyond its original goals. A simple data visualization might become a dashboard, then a public-facing platform, then a proposal for a much larger system.

New ideas can be exciting, but adding features without reconsidering your team's capacity can make it harder to deliver anything useful.

**What you can do:**

- **Revisit your original problem statement.** Is the new feature necessary to address the problem, or is it something that would be nice to have?
- **Define a realistic next milestone.** Focus on the smallest useful outcome your team can deliver and validate.
- **Separate current priorities from future ideas.** Keep a backlog of potential features without committing to building all of them.
- **Discuss tradeoffs openly.** Adding a new feature may mean delaying something else. Make those choices explicit.
- **Be willing to simplify.** A small tool that people actually use can be more valuable than an ambitious platform that never launches.

Sometimes the most productive decision a project can make is to stop adding features and focus on making its existing work useful.

## 6. When Volunteers Are Burning Out

Volunteer projects depend on people's enthusiasm, but enthusiasm alone cannot sustain a project indefinitely.

Burnout can emerge when a few contributors take on most of the work, project leads feel responsible for keeping everything moving, or volunteers feel pressured to meet expectations they never formally agreed to.

**What you can do:**

- **Normalize taking breaks.** Contributors should feel comfortable stepping away or reducing their involvement without having to justify themselves.
- **Share responsibilities.** Avoid allowing one person to become responsible for every meeting, decision, and deliverable.
- **Set realistic expectations.** A project that meets twice a month may move more slowly than a professional development team. That's okay.
- **Recognize different contributions.** Research, documentation, community outreach, testing, and onboarding are all valuable forms of work.
- **Celebrate progress beyond launches.** Completing a user interview, documenting a process, or helping someone make their first contribution are meaningful milestones too.

Project leads should also pay attention to their own capacity. Leadership doesn't mean being available all the time or taking on every unfinished task.

## 7. Knowing When to Ask for Help

Not every challenge can or should be resolved within a project team. Civic Tech DC organizers can help facilitate conversations, clarify expectations, and discuss whether a project needs additional support or a change in direction.

Consider reaching out when:

- A project has lost its lead or is struggling to maintain consistent participation.
- A community partnership is no longer functioning.
- A disagreement is preventing contributors from working together.
- The team is making commitments it may not be able to meet.
- A contributor raises concerns about conduct or the community environment.

You can contact the Civic Tech DC organizing team at **team@civictechdc.org**.

## Sometimes the Right Decision Is to Pause

Not every project needs to continue indefinitely. A project might achieve its original purpose, lose its community partner, or reach a point where no one has the capacity to maintain it.

Pausing or closing a project isn't necessarily a failure. What matters is making that decision thoughtfully, communicating with the people affected, and preserving useful work where possible.

If your team is considering whether to continue, transfer, or close a project, see our **[Maintaining or Closing a Project](/build/maintain-or-close/)** guide.

---

### A Final Thought

Volunteer-led civic tech work requires balancing ambition with the realities of people's time, relationships, and capacity. There won't always be a perfect solution when things go wrong.

The goal isn't to avoid every challenge. It's to build teams where people can communicate honestly, make decisions together, and step away when they need to without leaving others uncertain about what happens next.

Strong civic tech projects aren't just defined by what they build, but by how they work with the people who make that work possible.
