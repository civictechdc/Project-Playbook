<p>
  Building a civic tech project is only the first step. For a project to have an impact, people need to know it exists, understand why it matters, and have a way to get involved. 
  Growing your project means telling a clear story about the work and creating opportunities for others to engage.
</p>

<div class="civic-tech-section">

<h2 class="techsection-heading">Communications: Externally</h2>

<p>
  External communication is how you share your project with people outside your team. This includes the people your project serves, potential contributors, government partners, funders, and anyone else who may need to understand or support the work. Good external communication helps people understand what your project does, why it matters, and how they can get involved.
</p>

  </div>

  <p>
    It is extremely important to share progress and document decisions. Make sure contributors regularly share what they are working on and what needs to happen next.
  </p>

  <h3 id="share-progress">Share Progress</h3>

  <p>
    Make it easy for the team to see that work is moving forward. Here are some ideas:
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>
      Share progress updates after project nights or set a weekly Slack bot that ask for progress that you respond to
    </li>

    <li>
      Share new features
    </li>

    <li>
      Share interesting happenings in the space or research findings
    </li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    Progress updates are also a excellent way to recognize contributors. Shout out folks! A project can involve a lot of work that is not immediately visible, so taking time to acknowledge what people have accomplished helps contributors know their work is valuable.
  </p>

  <h3 id="document-decisions">Document Decisions</h3>

  <p>
    Write down important decisions as they happen. This is especially important when a decision changes the direction of the project, affects other contributors, or is something the team may need to revisit later. If decisions only live in someone’s memory, it becomes difficult for new contributors to understand how the project got to where it is or why something was built a certain way.
  </p>

  <p>
    You can document decisions in <strong>Slack or meeting notes</strong>. You do not need to record every conversation for every small decision, and you should not, because that takes up time. Instead, focus on the big things, such as if your team decides to choose a new technology or stop working on a feature; write down what happened and why.
  </p>

  <p>
    Focus on capturing:
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>
      <strong>What was decided</strong>: Clearly state the decision rather than just summarizing the conversation
    </li>

    <li>
      <strong>Who was involved</strong>: Include who participated in the decision or who is responsible for carrying it forward.
    </li>

    <li>
      <strong>What needs to happen next</strong>: Identify any follow-up tasks or changes that need to be made.
    </li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    Good documentation means that someone who joins the project later can understand what the team decided and what happened afterward without having to track down someone who was there. It also gives the existing team something to refer back to when questions come up later.
  </p>

  <p>
    A great project that nobody knows about won’t get used or contributed to. Building the technology is only part of the work; the people your project is meant to serve need to know it exists, and potential contributors need a clear way to find and participate in the project. Here are some resources to grow awareness of your project.
  </p>

  <h3 id="host-activities">Host your own Activities</h3>

  <p>
    You are welcome to host whatever type of events will help your project grow. This usually involves partnering with another organization for a demo or lightning talk — a low-lift way to get your project in front of an audience that’s already assembled and relevant.
  </p>

  :::notes
  In March 2026, WMATA reached out about any Civic Tech DC projects participating in an upcoming virtual Lunch and Learn series. The goal of the session is to expand the organization’s understanding of the various transit-oriented projects happening in the DC area. Ride Score DC presented their project. Opportunities like this come through the Civic Tech DC network — staying active in Slack and Project Nights is how you hear about them.
  :::

  <p>
    Civic Tech DC can also help you organize a dedicated working session, a public demo, or a user research workshop around your project. Please fill out this Google Form for the organizers with a brief description of what you’re trying to accomplish and roughly how many people you’d expect. Good formats that have worked for DC projects include:
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>
      <strong>Demo nights:</strong> Show what you’ve built to get feedback and attract new contributors
    </li>

    <li>
      <strong>Working sessions</strong>: Focused Project Night spin-offs where a small group tackles a specific problem
    </li>

    <li>
      <strong>User research meetups</strong>: Invite the people your project serves to test it and share their experience
    </li>
  </ul>

  <h3 id="visual-communications">Visual Communications</h3>

  <p>
    Visuals can make your project easier to understand and recognize. Think about how someone will encounter your project on Slack, social media, or the Civic Tech DC website.
  </p>

  <p>
    Keep visuals simple and consistent. Tools like Canva can help you create visual assets.
  </p>

  <h4 id="social-media">Use of Social Media</h4>

  <p>
    Social media can help you reach people who would not otherwise encounter your project through Project Night, Slack, or GitHub. You are welcome to use Civic Tech DC’s LinkedIn to help spread the word. LinkedIn is where much of DC’s professional community is active — a well-timed post about a milestone, a new partnership, or an open contributor role can reach people who wouldn’t otherwise find you.
  </p>

  <p>
    Please create a post in Canva, write a short description, and fill out this form. The organizers will review and schedule it.
  </p>

  <h4 id="logo">Create a Logo</h4>

  <p>
    A logo gives your project a recognizable identity and makes it easier to share across platforms consistently.
  </p>

  <p>
    Canva has free templates that are a good starting point. Aim for something simple that works at small sizes, like a Slack avatar, and larger sizes, like a slide header.
  </p>

  <p>
    You do not need to spend a lot of time creating the perfect logo. The goal is to make your project recognizable and give people something they can associate with the work.
  </p>

  <h4 id="website">Create a Website</h4>

  <p>
    A project website gives you a home that you own and control, somewhere to send partners and potential contributors that feels more permanent than a Slack channel or GitHub repo. Some ideas for your website can include:
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>Your mission statement</li>
    <li>Why the project was started</li>
    <li>What the project has accomplished</li>
    <li>How someone can get involved</li>
    <li>Links to GitHub and other project resources</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    AI tools can help you get a first version up quickly, though adding some personal touches show that you have spent time thinking about how you want to represent your project.
  </p>

  <p>
    <a href="https://astro.build/">Astro</a> is a good option for civic tech projects since it's free and open source. It is also well-suited to content-focused sites without requiring a lot of frontend experience.
  </p>

  <h4 id="pitch-deck">Have a Pitch Deck</h4>

  <p>
    A pitch deck gives you something polished to share when presenting at events, reaching out to partners, or onboarding new contributors who want the full picture.
  </p>

  <p>
    Google Slides or Canva are great platforms because they are easy to use and allow multiple contributors to collaborate and update the deck. Keep your pitch deck short and snappy. Focus on the most important information and use visuals whenever possible rather than filling slides with text.
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>What the project is and the problem it solves</li>
    <li>Who it’s for and who’s affected</li>
    <li>Where the project stands today</li>
    <li>How someone can get involved</li>
    <li>Who’s currently on the team</li>
  </ul>

  <h4 id="project-advocates">Encourage Others to Talk About It</h4>

  <p>
    Your team members are your best advocates. Make it easy for them by giving them a short, consistent way to describe the project, a sentence or two they can use at networking events, on LinkedIn, or when someone asks what they’ve been working on.
  </p>

  <p>
    Share this description in Slack and encourage people to make it their own. Contributors should understand the project well enough to explain <strong>what it does, and why it matters.</strong>
  </p>

  <div class="civic-tech-section">

<h2 class="techsection-heading">Communications: Internally</h2>

<p>
  Internal communication is how your project team stays connected and understands what is happening. As projects grow, it becomes harder for everyone to keep track of decisions, progress, and responsibilities. Regularly sharing updates and documenting important information helps make sure contributors know what others are working on and what needs to happen next.
</p>

  </div>

  <h3 id="tell-your-story">Tell Your Story</h3>

  <p>
    People connect with people, not software. When you talk about your project, start with the problem you are trying to solve and the people who experience it.
  </p>

  <p>
    A good explanation should make it easy for someone who has never heard of the project to understand:
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li><strong>What problem are you trying to solve?</strong></li>
    <li><strong>Who experiences this problem?</strong></li>
    <li><strong>Why does it matter?</strong></li>
    <li><strong>What is your project doing about it?</strong></li>
    <li><strong>What has happened because of the project so far?</strong></li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    Try to avoid leading with technical details. The technology is important, but it should help explain how you are addressing the problem rather than become the story itself.
  </p>

  <p>
    Here is a (made-up) example of how the same project might be described to different audiences:
  </p>

  <p><strong>Resident:</strong></p>

  <blockquote>
    <p>
      “DC Benefits Finder helps you figure out what utility assistance programs you may qualify for without having to search through multiple government websites. All you need to do is answer a few questions about your household, and the tool shows you programs that may be a good fit and where to apply.”
    </p>
  </blockquote>

  <p><strong>To a government agency:</strong></p>

  <blockquote>
    <p>
      “DC Benefits Finder helps residents identify utility assistance programs they may qualify for by bringing information from multiple government websites into a centralized system. The tool can help agencies and community organizations connect residents with the right resources while reducing the time staff spends directing people to different programs. Since launching, 2,000 residents have used the tool and three community organizations are using it.”
    </p>
  </blockquote>

  <h3 id="communicating-your-impact">Communicating Your Impact</h3>

  <p>
    People want to contribute, fund, and support a project that has tangible impact. But impact does not always mean a large number. The important thing is understanding what changed because of your project and using statistics and other evidence to help tell that story.
  </p>

  <p>
    Keep track of <strong>metrics and other evidence</strong> both to tell your story and to help you and your team understand whether the project is working. This could include:
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>How many people are using the project</li>
    <li>How many people have contributed</li>
    <li>How many organizations or partners are using it</li>
    <li>Feedback from people who use the project</li>
    <li>Changes the project has helped make</li>
    <li>New opportunities that came from the project</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    Impact can look different for every project. For one project, impact might mean thousands of people using a tool. For another, it might mean helping one community organization access information they could not previously use.
  </p>

  <p><strong>Next Steps:</strong></p>

  <ul>
    <li>
      <strong>Practice your story</strong> at Project Night either during the pitches or onboarding of new volunteers. Try explaining the project in 30 seconds to someone who has never heard of it.
    </li>

    <li>
      <strong>Start tracking your impact</strong> by documenting a few metrics or pieces of feedback that will help you understand whether the project is making a difference. Put it in a document for reference.
    </li>
  </ul>

  <h3 id="create-a-mission-statement">Create A Mission Statement</h3>

  <p>
    Write a clear, jargon-free one-paragraph description of what your project does and who it helps. This becomes your pitch at Project Nights, your description on your GitHub ReadMe, and your description on Slack. Make sure it answers: <em>what problem does this solve, for whom, and what stage is it at?</em>
  </p>

  <p>
    Try to write it so that someone who has never heard of your project can understand it in less than a minute. Avoid technical terms or organizational language that someone outside your project may not understand.
  </p>

  <p>
    Your mission statement can change as the project develops. Early on, you may be describing the problem you are exploring and what you hope to build. Later, you may be able to describe the people using the project and what you have built.
  </p>

  <p>
    One of the best things about a mission statement is that it helps you stay focused. As a project grows, it becomes easy to want to go in different directions or add new features. Revisiting your mission statement regularly helps you decide what is most useful for the project and what is not.
  </p>

  <p><strong>Next Steps:</strong></p>

  <ul>
    <li>
      Write your mission statement and share it with your team. Update your project description on GitHub, Slack, and the Civic Tech DC website so they reflect the current state of the project.
    </li>
  </ul>

<h2 class="techsection-heading" id="reach-your-audience">Reach Your Audience</h2>

  <p>
    A project can have a great tool, strong team, and meaningful mission and still struggle to grow if the right people do not know about it. Think about who needs to know about your project and what they would need to hear from you.
  </p>

  <p>
    You do not need to reach everyone. Focus on the people who are most important to the project right now.
  </p>

  <h3 id="community-members">Community Members</h3>

  <p>
    Start with the people your project is intended to serve. If you are building something for a community, spend time understanding what they actually need before deciding what to build or how to communicate about it.
  </p>

  <p>
    Whenever possible:
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>Talk directly with people who experience the problem you are trying to solve.</li>
    <li>Ask what they currently do instead.</li>
    <li>Listen for problems you may not have considered.</li>
    <li>Test your ideas before investing too much time in building them.</li>
    <li>Make it easy for people to give feedback.</li>
    <li>Close the loop and let people know what you did with their feedback.</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    The people affected by a problem should not only be an audience for your project. When possible, they should have a role in shaping it.
  </p>

  <h3 id="government">Government</h3>

  <p>
    Government agencies can be important partners for civic tech projects, particularly when a project works with public data, serves residents, or addresses a problem that government is already trying to solve.
  </p>

  <p>
    Start by understanding the agency and the problem from their perspective. A government partner may be interested in a project because it helps residents, reduces work for staff, makes better use of existing data, or helps them understand a problem they are struggling to address.
  </p>

  <p>
    When reaching out to government partners, be clear about what you are asking for. This might be feedback, access to data, an introduction to staff, a place to test a tool, or participation in a working session.
  </p>

  <p>
    You do not need to wait until your project is finished to talk to government. Early conversations can help you understand whether the problem you are solving is actually a priority and what constraints you need to consider.
  </p>

  <h3 id="funders">Funders</h3>

  <p>
    Funders need to quickly understand what problem you are solving, who is affected, and why your project is a good way to address it.
  </p>

  <p>
    A strong project story should make clear:
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>What the problem is</li>
    <li>Who is affected</li>
    <li>What your project does differently</li>
    <li>What you have accomplished so far</li>
    <li>What you could accomplish with additional resources</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    You do not need to turn every conversation into a funding pitch. A clear explanation of the problem and the work you are doing makes it easier for funders to understand where your project fits.
  </p>

  <p>
    If you are looking for funding, keep track of your accomplishments, partnerships, users, and other evidence of impact as the project develops. It is much easier to tell this story later if you have been documenting it along the way.
  </p>

  <h3 id="contributors">Contributors</h3>

  <p>
    Potential contributors need to be able to find your project and understand how they can help.
  </p>

  <p>
    Make sure your project has a clear description that explains what it does, why it matters, and what stage it is at. Your GitHub repository, website, Slack channel, and Project Night pitch should all tell roughly the same story.
  </p>

  <p>
    Give people a clear next step. Instead of simply saying that you are looking for volunteers, explain what someone can actually do.
  </p>

  <p>
    For example:
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>Help clean or analyze a dataset</li>
    <li>Test a new feature</li>
    <li>Conduct user research</li>
    <li>Design an interface</li>
    <li>Write documentation</li>
    <li>Help with outreach</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    People are much more likely to get involved when they can see where their skills fit.
  </p>

  <h3 id="press">Press</h3>

  <p>
    Journalists need a story, not just a description of your software.
  </p>

  <p>
    When talking to the press, focus on the problem your project is addressing, the people affected, and why the work is relevant now. A new feature may be interesting to your team, but the larger story might be what that feature makes possible for a community.
  </p>

  <p>
    Make it easy for someone to understand your project quickly. Have a short description, a few useful statistics or examples, and a clear way to contact someone on the team.
  </p>

  <p>
    If your project has a particularly interesting milestone, partnership, finding, or community story, let the Civic Tech DC organizers know. There may be opportunities to help amplify the work.
  </p>
